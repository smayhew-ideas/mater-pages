Point_01
An Unnamed Thing

I heard Jordan Klepper on The Daily Show say to a guest — "They're stealing our IP!"

Everyone laughed. I didn't.

Because what Klepper was reaching for isn't intellectual property. We have laws for that. What he was naming is a feeling — the feeling of being taken from — and he grabbed the nearest word that fit. The thing itself has no name yet. Its parts do — data, labor, knowledge — but the whole doesn't. No name, no singular identity. No identity, no value anyone has to account for. That's the whole problem, and this letter addresses it in that order.

Point_02
We Are the Source

It doesn't start as an economic argument, and it isn't a technology critique. It is an identity problem — something everyone in the industry keeps stepping over and calling the floor.

Everything artificial intelligence does — every answer, every sentence, every strategy, every nuance it approximates — was learned from us. From human knowledge, labor, experience, language, and judgment, built over the full history of civilization.

That is not a controversial claim. It is a fact that has not yet been treated as one.

A trillion-dollar industry stands on it, and it has no name. We are the source.

Point_03
What Cannot Be Owned Is Not Therefore Free

We talk about "training data" like it's gravel found by the side of the road. It isn't. It is the compressed record of human work — and more than that, it is the capacity that produced the work, scraped and poured into models at industrial scale.

Nobody owns that capacity. No one can. It belongs to no company, no government, no individual. And the industry's position, stated or not, is that what cannot be owned therefore costs nothing.

That is not economics. It is a category error. Many things cannot be owned and are not free: the atmosphere, the radio spectrum, a river's flow, a nation's trust in its own currency. Each is priced, governed or protected once someone names it and says what it is. Until then it is invisible, and invisible things get used for free.

What cannot be owned is not therefore free. It is unnamed.

Point_04
The Name

Here is the pattern, and it is old.

Oil was a nuisance in Pennsylvania salt wells until someone identified it as a resource. Then it had a price, then it had law, then it had an industry. A company's reputation was a feeling until accountants named it goodwill and gave it a line on the balance sheet. In every case the sequence was the same: identify, name, legislate, price. The law was blind to the thing, often hostile to it, until the name settled into public use. Policy does not recognize what it cannot say.

Naming in this sense is not coining. Pieces of this thing already have words: training data, human capital, collective intelligence, the commons. Each describes a part, a population or a use. None names the whole as something real enough to be claimed and held to account. That is what a name does once we agree to it. It says: this exists, it has value, and someone is answerable for it.

We are at the first step with the largest resource ever put to use, and we have not taken it. We have bottled humanity. We haven't labeled the bottle.

So let me label it. The thing AI runs on — the whole human capacity for perception, judgment, taste, cultural fluency, emotional calibration, the accumulated knowledge of every person who ever explained anything to anyone — I call [MATER]. The brackets are deliberate: they mark the empty slot on the ledger, with the name finally written in. The source. Latin for mother; the root of matrix and, by the old account, of matter and material. A word for source that is itself sourced from what it names.

Mater is not a dataset. Datasets are what it leaves behind. It is not "the public" or "humanity" — those are populations, and populations have no standing. It is the one input in the entire AI supply chain with no substitute and no way to reproduce it. Every other input — compute, power, capital, talent — has alternatives and a line on the books. This one has neither.

The identity of AI towers over the identity of the source. The derivative has a brand. The original doesn't.

The name is almost trivial once it's said. That's the point. It's the saying that has been missing.

Point_05
Under the Artifact

AI didn't violate intellectual property. It did something more fundamental. It went under it.

IP protects artifacts: specific songs, specific articles, specific code. AI learned what IP doesn't cover — the judgment that made the song work, the fluency that makes journalism land, the pattern recognition that writes elegant code, the taste that composes an image, and the ephemera underneath all of it: the pause, the shrug, the five different things a person can mean by "fine." The real value was never the artifact. It was the capacity underneath. That capacity is Mater, and the industry stands on it.

Everything a model produces can be copied endlessly and cheaply. That is its design. The thing it was trained on cannot be reproduced by anything, including the model. Train models on their own output and they degrade; researchers call it model collapse. There is no second source to switch to if this one gets expensive. That is the asymmetry: the derivative is infinitely copyable and fully accounted for; the original is singular and appears nowhere. In any other market, no one would price a derivative without pricing the underlying. Here the underlying is Mater, and it is on no one's books.

And the original is not an archive. It is live. Every correction, every better prompt, every piece of difficult creative work uploaded, every translation that took a lifetime to understand — the product sharpens, the valuation rises, and the source is drawn on again. The millionth call draws on it the same as the first. This is not depletion; Mater is not used up. It is dependence, continuous and total, and dependence is what the rest of the economy pays for.

Point_06
The Accounting Gap

Look at the books.

Every AI company has a line item for compute. For electricity. For data centers. For engineering talent. For real estate. They account for everything except the one input with no substitute.

It's like an oil company with no line item for oil.

This isn't morality. It's mechanics. An oil company pays a royalty on every barrel, to whoever holds the claim. Here no one holds the claim, because the thing has no name, and you cannot book a cost to no one.

It isn't hidden. Everyone can see it. It stays off the books because there was no name to put on the line — and because putting it there would change the math.

The gap can be measured, and the loop can be drawn. The measurement is a simple index built from four public signals — new human questions asked in public, the share of the web still open, the human share of new writing, human visits to Wikipedia — each scored against its own level before 2022. After 2022 the average falls from 1.0 to about 0.56. The Model draws that record, then draws two scenarios from here, as illustrations rather than forecasts. In one, the loop closes by design: the source is accounted for and paid into, and it recovers. In the other, it closes by fracture: models train on their own output, trust becomes a premium sold back behind walls, and the source thins toward nothing — and so does everything built on it. → Figure 1: The Scissors · → The Model

Point_07
A Framework to Test

For the first time in history, we have a system that runs on all of us, serves all of us, and could return value to all of us — not as utopia, as accounting. Naming is the first step. It is not the last, and a name with nothing behind it is easy to agree with and ignore. So here is a framework — specific on purpose, so it can be tested where it is weakest.

The instruments are not new. ASCAP has collected for songwriters since 1914. Alaska has paid a dividend on a shared resource since 1982. In the last year economists, market designers and policy architects have proposed versions of them for AI. What none of them has is the entity. They are designing a payment to no one in particular. This framework starts from the entity.

Tuition — for the past. What training has already drawn from the whole, settled once into an interest-bearing fund. This is the hardest joint. Payment for past use needs either new law or a negotiated settlement, and the courts are only beginning to decide. In September, the first federal appeals court to rule found that training an AI tool on copyrighted material was not fair use, and recognized a market for licensing training data. Generative AI is still undecided.

Source Cost — for the present. A fixed share of the revenue AI models produce, every year, because the source is a live flow and a flow cannot be paid off once. The joints here are definitions: what counts as AI revenue when it is bundled into other products, who collects from companies that operate everywhere, and what to do about open models that earn nothing directly.

The Trust — for the whole. This is where the framework becomes real: the money has to reach the source.

It takes a private body and public law together, and the pairing already exists. Congress created a license for digital radio, a federal board sets the rates, and an independent nonprofit, SoundExchange, collects the royalties and pays artists directly. It is governed by artists and rights owners, and it has paid out more than $10 billion. In 1992 Congress went further and put a royalty on digital recording devices themselves, sent to the musicians and songwriters whose work those devices copied. A technology that draws on human work has paid the people it draws on before.

I can't design that body. But any design worth the name has to meet five conditions. The money reaches people. It is governed independently of those who pay in. Every flow is published. Membership is universal. And part of it is invested back in the source. Source Cost pays for using it; this pays for keeping it — capital spending on the one asset everything else runs on, in the capacities no model has yet seen — because a source that is funded grows.

Under all three sits the joint that matters most. ASCAP worked because copyright gave songwriters a right to license. Alaska's dividend works because the state holds title to the oil. Mater has no recognized right yet. The framework needs one: either law that recognizes the source as a party, or a compact the industry enters because the return is real.

And the return is real. We are not only AI's source. We are also its only customer: the loop begins with people and ends with people. A source that is accounted for keeps producing. Models trained on their own output degrade; they need a steady supply of new human work, and that supply is already closing. In a single year, more than a quarter of the most actively maintained sources in one widely used training set restricted AI use. Paying in pieces, through lawsuits, costs more and settles less: one AI company has agreed to pay $1.5 billion over pirated books alone. A funded source also grows. Capacities that no one could afford to develop become inputs no model has seen. The whole industry gains from that. No single company gains by paying alone, which is why it takes a shared instrument and not good intentions.

The scale is the point. Price the source at one dollar per American per year — an abstract figure, chosen only to show volume — and it comes to about $340 million: less than two frontier training runs, under two percent of one AI company's annual revenue, about half of one percent of NVIDIA's profit in a single quarter. The dollar is not a price. It is a way to see how much of us is in this: so much that even a dollar a head is a fund worth governing.

Keep building. Go to Mars if you like. Just close the loop before you leave.

Every joint named here is where I expect this to be attacked. Good. That is where it needs the people this letter is written for.

Point_08
We Are Not Oil

I come to this from thirty years as a creative director — structural work for IBM, the Gates Foundation, the Rockefeller Foundation, NAACP Legal Defense Fund. I use these tools every day — I used them to help write this letter — and I think they are extraordinary. That is why this matters to me. My work has always been visual, but I was never hired for decoration. I was hired because organizations could not see what was wrong with their own systems. The first job, every time, was to name the thing in the room and make it visible, so that everyone could finally point at it. Once a thing is named and seen, it can't be unseen. That is how change starts.

I have never seen a pattern this large go this unnamed.

Klepper said they were stealing our IP. But there is no they. The people who built these systems are part of the source too; their work is in the models alongside everyone else's. No one can say Mater doesn't exist — the technology proves it does. No one can say it has no value — it is being monetized. What's left is terms, and terms are written on the books. You cannot write terms for something that has no name.

The earth didn't invoice us. The whales didn't negotiate. The oil didn't unionize. But we are not oil. We are the source. And the source has to be accounted for.

None of this is finished. It needs stress-testing from people who do this for a living: economists who can attach it to the literature on knowledge externalities and public goods, policy architects who can write the brief a senator's staff can act on, lawyers who have built collective-rights instruments and know what makes them enforceable, and people with institutional standing who can test the framework at its joints and say whether it holds.

I am not looking for agreement. I am looking for the people whose rigor makes it stick.

If this letter described something you already felt but hadn't named, write to me. Not to endorse it. To add what I can't see from here. To find the flaw I haven't found. To bring the credential I don't have.

The source doesn't need a movement. It needs a name, and then a line item, and then the people who know how to make line items permanent.
